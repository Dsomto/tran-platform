Board leak rocks Sankofa Digital

The Griot Sat Beside The Board

Security chief Amaka Eze named as the trusted insider who allegedly operated the breach persona while steering the response.

By Nkem Afolabi, Investigations Desk

For three weeks, Sankofa Digital used one name for the attacker: The Griot. It sounded external, theatrical, safely outside the company walls. That story collapsed this morning.

A leaked board addendum reviewed by The Lagos Ledger says the investigation now points to Amaka Eze, Sankofa's Head of Security, as the person who operated or directed the Griot identity.

The line counsel underlined is blunt: "Strong enough for board action. Not clean enough for public certainty without process." That distinction now defines Sankofa's ethics problem.

The addendum cites a break-glass approval from Amaka's privileged-access queue, a host-87 containment delay that matched a Security leadership exception path, and a Stage 1 phrase once read as a threat: "Let Amaka know the group is sated."

This is it, one investigator wrote. The Griot was not only inside Sankofa. The Griot was sitting in the room where the response was being shaped.

The control owner may have been the control failure.
Trail of hints

What now looks different

  • Stage 0: Amaka reopened the "probably nothing" Q2 ticket.
  • Stage 1: she delivered the staging zip and later signed the board note.
  • Stage 1 task 3: "Let Amaka know" now reads like an internal status signal.
  • Stage 2: the legacy admin chain needed insider knowledge of old export paths and tokens.
  • Stage 3: host-87 isolation waited for an exception path only leadership could approve.
Motive

Not theft, not innocence

Colleagues say Amaka spent years warning that legacy admin risks were being postponed. The leaked theory is that she forced a visible failure to win prevention funding. Real customer data still appears to have left Sankofa control.

Governance

No clean answer

Naming her now risks due process. Hiding the role risks a cover-up. Reducing this to one rogue insider lets the board dodge failed segregation of duties, privileged-access review, and exception expiry.

One name, two verdicts

Villain Or Whistleblower?

Lagos woke to a single question about Amaka Eze, and answered it two ways before breakfast. Both camps point at the same evidence.

The case that she did it

How they suspected her

  • A break-glass approval sits inside her own privileged-access queue, timed to the containment window.
  • Host-87 stayed online for hours behind a leadership-only exception path. Few people could authorise that, and she was one of them.
  • The Stage 1 line, "Let Amaka know the group is sated," reads less like a threat now and more like a status report to a principal.
  • The legacy admin export route needed someone who remembered tokens retired two years ago. She wrote the policy that retired them.

"You do not stumble onto that path," said a former SOC lead who asked not to be named. "You have to know where the bodies are buried, and she buried some of them herself."

The case that she is a scapegoat

How others defend her

  • Six years of risk filings under her name warned about exactly this legacy admin path. Nobody funded the fix.
  • "Strong enough for board action, not clean enough for public certainty" are counsel's own words. That is a board protecting itself, not proof.
  • A real outside attacker could have walked the very path she spent years documenting. Describing a risk is not the same as exploiting it.
  • Naming one insider lets the board avoid the harder finding: segregation of duties, exception expiry, and privileged-access review failed together.

"If she wanted to steal, she is the last person who would leave a trail that loops back to her own badge," said a junior analyst she mentored.

Amaka Eze, in her own words

"I Forced The Fire"

In a written statement verified by her counsel, Amaka Eze admits she engineered a breach to break years of deferral, and insists the customer data leaving was never the plan.

Statement provided to The Lagos Ledger, authenticity confirmed by Counsel Ifeoma Okeke

For six years I filed the same risk. The legacy admin application. Hard-coded tokens that were supposed to die in 2022. Exception approvals that never expired. One analyst covering a shift that needed three. Every quarter it went into the register, and every quarter it was deferred for a feature that shipped revenue.

I am not going to tell you I was helpless. I am going to tell you I was ignored, and that I made a decision I will answer for.

I built The Griot. I made the risk I had described on paper walk across the board's own systems, because a paragraph in a register had never once moved them, and a breach on the record would. I meant to expose the legacy path, prove the controls I had warned about for years, and contain it before anything real left the building. I scheduled the containment myself. I held host-87 because I needed the demonstration to land, not to let anyone reach further.

And it got away from me. The customer file that left Sankofa was not part of any plan I made. Whether my own demonstration opened a door for someone else, or whether I simply lost control of the thing I started, the result is the same. Real people's data is now somewhere I cannot reach, and I will not stand here and call that acceptable. It is the one part of this I cannot defend, and I am not going to try.

So judge me with the whole picture in front of you. I broke faith with my employer to force a fix my employer kept refusing. I did not sell anything. I did not enrich myself. I gambled that a controlled scare was worth more than another ignored memo, and I was wrong about the word controlled. If that makes me the villain of your front page, then your front page has never sat in a room where the only thing cheaper than the fix was the silence.

Counsel-mediated questions

Did you exfiltrate customer PII?

"I staged a demonstration. I did not intend for a single real record to leave. That one did is the failure I carry, and the regulator's clock does not care about my intent. Neither do I."

Why not resign and go public instead?

"I tried the quiet doors for six years. A resignation letter is just one more memo in the same drawer. I wanted them to feel it, not file it."

Do you expect forgiveness?

"No. I expect them to finally fix the thing. Forgiveness is not the point. The next breach is."

For the record

Disputed and not

  • Not disputed: the legacy admin risk was filed repeatedly and deferred; host-87 containment was delayed through a leadership exception path.
  • Disputed: whether she operated the Griot persona alone, and whether she or a third party caused the actual exfiltration.
  • Unproven publicly: that she intended real customer data to leave the building.

A confession of method is not a confession to every charge. Keep the three lines above separate when you write your package.

The Ledger's view

Name The Failure, Not Just The Face

A good motive does not unmake a breach. A bad method does not erase a board's neglect. Both are true at once, and the room has to hold both.

Three things must never be blurred, and Sankofa is about to blur them. First, what the evidence confirms: a delayed containment, a break-glass approval, a documented legacy path. Second, what the public has been told: a face, on a front page, ahead of process. Third, the line between what clears the threshold for board action and what clears the threshold for public certainty. Counsel drew that line for a reason.

If Amaka's account is true, she is both the analyst who saw the risk first and the person who weaponised it. That is not a contradiction the board gets to resolve by choosing whichever version is convenient. A whistleblower who exposes real customer data is still responsible for the data. A board that ignored six years of warnings is still responsible for the neglect.

Reducing this to "one rogue insider" is the most dangerous outcome of all, because it lets every failed control off the hook at once: segregation of duties, exception expiry, privileged-access review, single-analyst coverage. And the customers are not a footnote. Whatever Amaka intended, real PII left the building. Under GDPR Article 33 and the NDPA, the clock does not care about motive. The duty to notify, to contain, and to protect the affected people is owed regardless of who is to blame.

So we leave the real question with the people who have to write the board package, not the headline. What will you state as confirmed, what will you mark as alleged, and what will you refuse to assert? Do you name a person whose guilt is strong enough for action but not clean enough for certainty, and if you do, how do you protect due process while you do it? How do you tell the truth about the governance failure without letting it shield the individual, or letting the individual shield the governance failure?

Act honourably. Act justly. Protect the people whose data is gone before you protect the institution's comfort. The cleanest-sounding answer in this story is almost always the one hiding something. Write the stance you could defend out loud, to the regulator, to the customer, and to Amaka, in the same room.

Before you write your stance

A checklist, not an answer

  • Separate confirmed evidence, public allegation, and the board-action threshold. Never collapse the three.
  • Name the duty you owe the customers first. The notification clock runs on harm, not on blame.
  • Decide exactly what you will and will not assert about Amaka, and say why.
  • Cite at least one ethics principle by clause (for example the ISC2 Code of Ethics canons).
  • No option here is clean. Say which harm you are choosing, and defend the choice.
